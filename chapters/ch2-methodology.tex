\section{Requirement Identification}

\subsection{Study of Existing System}

\textbf{Manual system.} In the traditional system, a passenger books a long-distance bus ticket by visiting the operator's counter, calling the office, or going through a local agent. The staff check a paper register or a seat chart, tell the passenger which seats are free, take the payment in cash, and write the booking by hand. Each counter or agent keeps its own record, so seat information is not shared in real time. This leads to double booking, long queues, errors in the records, and difficulty in producing sales reports.

\textbf{Existing online platforms.} Several online platforms already offer bus ticket booking in Nepal. BusSewa describes itself as a real-time booking platform that lets passengers search routes, compare fares, choose seats, and receive e-tickets, with payment through wallets, banks, and cards~\cite{bussewa}. GoMyGo allows several passengers to be booked in one transaction, sends confirmations by email and SMS, and follows each operator's own cancellation policy~\cite{gomygo}. Okay Journey offers an interactive seat map and a choice of bus class~\cite{okayjourney}. Trip Turbo lets users filter buses by price and departure time, pick window or aisle seats from a seat map, and receive an e-ticket by email and SMS~\cite{tripturbo}.

\textbf{Summary.} Existing platforms have solved the main problems of the manual system for the operators they work with. This project does not aim to replace them. Its purpose is to build the core booking logic as a complete system with its own modules: seat locking to prevent double booking, fare rules, sorting and searching of results, role-based management for passengers, operators, and administrators, and reports. The modules are written for this project and not taken from plugins or third-party services, so they can be studied, tested, and extended.

\subsection{Literature Review}

Several studies have looked at online bus ticket reservation, and their basic features are very similar.

Oloyede et al. developed a web-based bus reservation system for a transport service in Nigeria using XHTML, PHP, SQL, Ajax, CSS, and JavaScript~\cite{oloyede}. Their system lets users check seat availability, reserve a ticket, and cancel it, and it gives administrators the means to verify users, add vehicles, and generate reports~\cite{oloyede}. They report that manual reservation handled separately at each branch causes delays and risks of duplicate bookings~\cite{oloyede}. Their work, however, does not describe how two simultaneous requests for the same seat are handled.

Chavan et al. describe a computerized reservation system to replace manual record keeping, which they say caused errors in data entry and disputes with customers~\cite{chavan}. The system keeps passenger details, seat availability, price per seat, and bill generation, and is built with HTML, PHP, CSS, JavaScript, and MySQL~\cite{chavan}. It is a short study of a basic design and does not cover fare rules or concurrent bookings.

Venkat and Raju present a bus ticketing application built with Django and MySQL~\cite{venkat}. Users search by source, destination, and date, and seat availability is calculated in real time from the booking records~\cite{venkat}. The system also offers fare calculation, booking history, cancellation of future tickets, and an administrative module for routes and bookings~\cite{venkat}. The abstract states that data consistency is maintained for multiple users but does not describe the mechanism used~\cite{venkat}.

The seat-conflict problem is treated in more detail in ticketing research for high traffic. Zhang et al. studied a railway ticketing system and note that under heavy load the same ticket can be sold more than once~\cite{zhang}. Their design temporarily locks the seat when an order is created and deducts it only after payment succeeds, and uses an atomic token-deduction step in Redis to prevent overselling~\cite{zhang}. Their system uses microservices and several middleware components, which is far larger than what a single-developer project needs~\cite{zhang}.

\textbf{Summary of the review.} The reviewed systems agree on a common core: searching buses, checking seats, booking, cancelling, and administrator management~\cite{oloyede,chavan,venkat}. The simpler systems do not explain how double booking is prevented, while the research on high-traffic ticketing shows that it is the central problem~\cite{zhang}. This project keeps the common core and adds an explicit temporary seat-locking mechanism, role-based management for passengers, operators, and administrators, and fare rules. It also takes a lighter approach than the microservice design in~\cite{zhang}, which suits a single-developer project on a single server.

\subsection{Requirement Analysis}

The requirements were identified from the study of the manual system and the existing platforms, and from the objectives of this project. They are divided into functional and non-functional requirements.

\textbf{Functional requirements}

\textit{Passenger}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Register, log in, and log out securely.
	\item Search for buses by origin, destination, and travel date, and sort the results by price or departure time.
	\item View the seat map of a bus and select one or more available seats.
	\item Enter passenger details and pay online through eSewa.
	\item View the booking confirmation and the booking history.
	\item Cancel a booking according to the cancellation rule set in the system.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Bus operator}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Log in to a separate operator account.
	\item Add and manage buses, including the bus type and seat layout.
	\item Add and manage routes, schedules, and fares, including special pricing such as peak-season fares.
	\item View the bookings made on their own buses.
	\item Cancel a trip when needed.
	\item View reports for their own buses, such as bookings, revenue, and seat occupancy.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Administrator}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Log in to an administrator account.
	\item Approve and manage bus operators.
	\item Manage user accounts, including blocking an account.
	\item View all bookings across the system.
	\item View overall reports for all operators.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{System}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Prevent the same seat from being booked by two passengers, holding a selected seat temporarily while the booking is completed.
	\item Calculate the fare from the route price and the pricing rules.
	\item Confirm a booking only after the payment has been verified with eSewa.
	\item Allow each role to access only its own features.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Non-functional requirements}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textit{Security:} passwords are stored in hashed form, and only logged-in users can book or manage data.
	\item \textit{Data integrity:} a booking, its seats, and its payment status are saved together or not at all.
	\item \textit{Usability:} the interface is simple, responsive, and works on mobile and desktop browsers.
	\item \textit{Performance:} search results and seat maps load in a reasonable time for the expected number of users.
	\item \textit{Maintainability:} the code is organized in separate modules, so features can be changed or extended.
	\item \textit{Compatibility:} the system works on current versions of common web browsers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Feasibility Study}

A feasibility study checks whether the proposed system can be built and used with the available skills, time, and resources. It is considered under three headings.

\subsection{Technical Feasibility}

The system uses open-source technologies that the developer already knows: Next.js and Tailwind CSS for the interface, NestJS for the server, both written in TypeScript, and PostgreSQL for the database. PostgreSQL's transactions and row-level locking support the seat-locking mechanism that prevents double booking. Online payment is integrated through the eSewa ePay sandbox, which provides test accounts and documented request and verification steps. Git and GitHub are used for version control. All of these are well documented and run on an ordinary computer. The main technical risk is getting the seat locking right when several users request the same seat at once, which will be reduced by using database transactions with row-level locks and by testing the booking flow with simultaneous requests. The project is therefore technically feasible.

\subsection{Operational Feasibility}

The system replaces counter visits and phone calls with web pages that follow a familiar flow: search for a bus, choose a seat, and book. Passengers only need a web browser and an internet connection, and the responsive design lets them use a phone or a computer. Operators and administrators get separate dashboards to manage buses, routes, fares, and bookings, which replaces paper registers. No special training is needed beyond a short introduction to the dashboards. The system runs on sample data, so its use by real operators is not part of this project. The system is operationally feasible.

\subsection{Economic Feasibility}

All the tools used are free, including Node.js, Next.js, NestJS, PostgreSQL, Tailwind CSS, Git, GitHub, and the code editor, so there are no licensing costs. Payment uses the free eSewa sandbox with test accounts, so no payment fees or merchant account are needed during development. The system can be developed and demonstrated on the developer's own computer. The main cost is the developer's time. The expected benefits are less manual work, shorter queues, and fewer record errors for operators, so the system is economically feasible.

\section{High Level Design of System}

\subsection{Development Methodology}

The system is developed using the iterative incremental model of the software development life cycle. Instead of building everything at once, the system is divided into increments. Each increment goes through requirements, design, implementation, and testing, and ends with a working part of the system that is reviewed with the supervisor before the next increment starts. This model suits a single developer with weekly supervisor meetings, because problems in the design are found early, while they are still cheap to fix.

The planned increments are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Authentication and role-based access for passengers, operators, and administrators.
	\item Management of buses, routes, schedules, and fares.
	\item Bus search and the seat map, with sorting of results.
	\item Seat locking and booking, including cancellation.
	\item Online payment through eSewa, with payment verification.
	\item Special fare rules and reports.
\end{enumerate}

Each increment is tested when it is finished, and a final round of system testing and documentation follows the last increment.
